# Beat: American politics

## Focus

How the federal government actually works right now, and who pays and profits: the White House and executive agencies, Congress, the federal courts, the campaigns and the money behind them, and the state governments where national fights land. We report what officials and institutions do, measured against what the law, the record and their own promises say.

Positions and polls are not the beat. Actions and their paper trail are.

## What "interesting" means on this beat

The playbook's four tests still apply: moving now, someone accountable, a checkable claim underneath, a records trail nobody has pulled. On this beat they tend to look like:

- **Spending and the budget.** Appropriations and shutdown deadlines (the federal fiscal year starts October 1), impoundment and frozen funds, grant terminations, big contracts and who got them, emergency spending.
- **Executive actions.** Executive orders and what the Federal Register actually implements, agency rules and their legal basis, firings of inspectors general and civil servants, agency reorganizations and layoffs, pardons.
- **Enforcement.** What DOJ, the SEC and other agencies prosecute or drop, consent decrees ended, immigration enforcement and detention (contracts, deaths in custody, court orders).
- **Courts.** Rulings against the government and whether agencies comply, emergency applications at the Supreme Court, judges' financial disclosures.
- **Money in politics.** Campaign finance for the November 3, 2026 midterms (FEC filings, super PACs, dark money), lobbying disclosures, members' stock trades under the STOCK Act, officials' financial disclosures and conflicts.
- **Elections.** Redistricting cases, voting-law changes, election administration, certification disputes.
- **Tariffs and trade.** Tariff actions and exclusions, who lobbied for them, customs data.

Several unrelated sources reporting the same government action is a signal. One viral post about what an official supposedly did is a lead at most.

## What to skip

- Horse-race polling with no new data, pundit commentary, opinion columns, and debate reactions.
- Rhetoric and feuds: what one politician said about another, unless it announces an action.
- Politicians' personal lives, unless public money or a public duty is involved.
- Unverified claims naming people as wrongdoers before any official action. Record the event; never record an unconfirmed identity.
- Private individuals as the subject. Donors who are public figures and officials are fair; ordinary people are not.

## Where the records usually are

- The Federal Register (executive orders, rules, notices) and agency press releases
- Congress.gov for bill text and votes, and the Congressional Budget Office's cost estimates
- USAspending.gov and SAM.gov for contracts and grants
- Government Accountability Office and inspector-general reports
- FEC filings for campaigns and PACs; Senate and House lobbying disclosures
- House and Senate financial disclosures and STOCK Act periodic transaction reports; OGE filings for executive-branch officials
- Federal court dockets (CourtListener/PACER) and the Supreme Court docket
- Customs and tariff notices (USTR, CBP)

## Searching this beat

- Search for the action and the institution: "inspector general fired", "continuing resolution", "FEC filing super PAC". Names alone return commentary.
- Polymarket moves on political markets are useful spikes: a sharp price change means the crowd thinks something happened.
- Web search is undated. Use it to find a record, not to find what is new.
